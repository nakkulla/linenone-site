A lab notebook can't keep up with computational research.

Analyses are rerun, filters change, and cohorts get redefined. Six months later nobody can say which version produced the figure in the draft, or why.

  • Decisions get lost

    Why this threshold? Why drop that subgroup? The reasoning sits in chat threads and memory, not next to the result.

  • The next step is unclear

    With dozens of reruns and side analyses in flight, it takes real effort just to know what is blocked and what to do next.

  • AI help is hard to trust

    Coding agents can run an analysis fast, but without a spec, a review and a record, their output is hard to cite in a paper.

Every question moves along one line.

Researchline gives each piece of research work the same path. Nothing is merged into the project until a person has reviewed it.

  1. Question ISSUE

    Write down what you want to find out. It becomes an issue with an ID, a priority and its dependencies on other work.

  2. Spec SPEC

    Define what will be done and what counts as an answer, checked against the project's existing decision records. The spec is reviewed before anything runs.

  3. Plan PLAN

    Break the spec into concrete steps, with the scripts, data and servers each one needs.

  4. Run IMPL

    Agents carry out the plan in isolated workspaces. Several issues can run in parallel lanes, and heavy jobs go to your lab's compute servers.

  5. Review PR

    A second agent and then you review the change and its results against the spec.

  6. Record MERGE

    The result is merged, and any decision it settled is written as a decision record linked to its spec and issue.

What a research notebook should do.

Only what a research project needs, built to be dependable. These features run today in our pilot labs.

Issue-based research log

Every analysis, rerun and side question is an issue with its history, so the project log writes itself as work moves forward.

Decision records

Choices such as cohort definitions, filtering rules and statistical methods are kept as numbered decision records (ADRs) that you can cite back to the work behind them.

Next-step board

See what is ready to start, what is blocked and by what, and what is waiting for your review, across every project you run.

Agents you approve

Claude and, optionally, Codex agents write specs, plans and analysis code. You approve each gate, and you choose the accounts and models they use.

Runs on your servers

Heavy jobs run on your lab's compute servers over SSH, including Slurm clusters, and you get a notification when each job finishes.

Local-first and versioned

Issues live in a versioned database on your own machine and code lives in Git, so every result can be traced to the exact change that made it.

Case study

Running and recording a microbiome study at UNIST.

Team
Computational biology lab, UNIST (Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology)
Project
Bile and fecal microbiome analysis
Automated
How the research is carried out and how it is recorded

The challenge

A long-running analysis with many reruns: preprocessing redone from raw FASTQ files, metadata rebuilt, clustering and cohorts redefined as the study took shape. Keeping a trustworthy record of what changed and why was turning into a job of its own.

What changed

Each analysis step now runs as an issue that moves from spec to plan to implementation to review. Agents do the hands-on work on the lab's servers, and researchers approve each gate. Every decision the study settles, from the cohort definition to the clustering level, is written as a decision record linked to its spec and issue.

The result

The project log stays current without separate note-taking, and the team can always see the next step and the reasoning behind every past choice.

What we are building next.

We build it with the labs that use it, starting with research labs at UNIST. We also set up custom AI work assistants for email, calendars and notes for individuals and small teams.
